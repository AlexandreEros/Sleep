\section{Dream Modalities across the Tree of Life}\label{sec:comparison}

The comparative evidence for sleep's universality naturally leads to questions about the phenomenology of dreaming across species. Do all animals dream? If so, do they dream in similar ways, or are their dreams tailored to their unique sensory modalities and ecological niches?

The ``overfitted brain'' hypothesis provides a powerful framework for understanding why different animals dream differently. If dreams function to ``de-overfit'' the brain and generalize learning, then the content of those dreams should be specific to whatever skills and senses are most critical and most over-trained for that species.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rodents and Spatial Replay:} As noted in Section~\ref{sec:nrem_consolidation}, rodents replay hippocampal place-cell activity from their waking explorations, effectively re-running mazes in their sleep \citep{wilson1994reactivation}. This aligns perfectly with the hypothesis, as spatial navigation is a critical, high-stakes skill for a rodent, and ``replaying'' these routes even in bursts or in reverse would serve to consolidate and generalize that spatial memory.
    \item \textbf{Bats and Acoustic-Spatial Replay:} This principle extends to other mammals with unique sensory specializations. Bats, for example, possess standard mammalian REM/NREM sleep cycles. Studies have shown that their hippocampus also ``replays'' flight trajectories during sleep \citep{omer2025batreplay}. Given that their most critical and computationally demanding skill is echolocation, it is inferred that their dreams are likely ``echo-spatial remixes'' of recent flights. The ``bizarre'' or ``odd reverberations'' in such dreams would serve the ``overfitting'' function perfectly, acting as adversarial noise to help the bat generalize its echolocation skills to new, unseen environments \citep{suga2003}.
    \item \textbf{Insects and Simpler Organisms:} The question of dreaming is meaningful even in simpler animals. Sleep has been clearly identified in insects like \textit{Drosophila} (fruit flies) \citep{tian2023drosophila} and even in nematode worms \citep{lesku2016evolutionary}. These organisms also require sleep for proper brain function and memory consolidation, suggesting the ``offline'' maintenance function is deeply conserved \citep{tian2023drosophila}. While we cannot know their subjective experience, the core biological need for an offline consolidation phase exists.
    \item \textbf{A Marine Mammal Exception (Dolphins):} The universality of REM-like dreaming, however, has a critical exception: cetaceans (dolphins and whales) \citep{lyamin2021cetacean}. These marine mammals have evolved unihemispheric slow-wave sleep (USWS), a state where one half of the brain sleeps while the other remains ``awake''. This adaptation is likely necessary to manage breathing in the water and maintain vigilance against predators \citep{ridgway2002, rattenborg2000}. Consequently, cetaceans exhibit ``little to no REM sleep'', as the total-body paralysis and sensory disconnection of REM sleep would be incompatible with their aquatic needs \citep{lyamin2021cetacean}. This suggests that while sleep itself is universal, the architecture of sleep (and the associated phenomenology of immersive dreaming) can be radically adapted or suppressed by extreme evolutionary pressures.
\end{itemize}

If dreams evolved to fight overfitting, their ``feel'' should mirror each species' dominant learning channel and their vividness should scale with how much REM-like, bilaterally integrated sleep they can afford.

The table~\ref{tab:tree_of_life} requires a significant methodological caveat. The study of animal consciousness, particularly the subjective experience of dreaming, is severely constrained by the ``language impediment'' \- the fact that non-human animals cannot provide the verbal, subjective reports that form the basis of human oneirology \citep{nielsen2003typical}. This necessitates a cautious, framework-based approach to inferring ``dream-like'' states.

One ``hard-stance'' framework, articulated by Manger and Siegel, defines dreaming strictly by its physiological correlate in humans: REM sleep \citep{manger2020}. By this criterion, if a species lacks robust, bilateral REM sleep, it is unlikely to experience vivid, narrative-like dream mentation \citep{manger2020}. The critical test case is the cetacean (dolphins, whales). As noted, cetaceans evolved unihemispheric SWS and demonstrate ``little to no REM sleep'' to avoid paralysis while breathing in water \citep{lyamin2021cetacean}. Therefore, under this physiological framework, cetaceans are the ``least likely mammalian group to experience vivid dream mentation'' \citep{manger2020}.

An alternative framework, advanced by philosophers like David Peña-Guzmán, argues against ``beginning from the human case'' and instead views consciousness on a continuum \citep{penaguzman2022, penaguzman2022_review}. This approach infers dreaming from homologous, rather than identical, evidence. This includes: (1) observable behaviors, like a dog twitching or ``running'' in its sleep \citep{penaguzman2022}; (2) neurobiological replays, such as rodents replaying maze-running place-cell sequences or songbirds replaying ``song'' circuits \citep{lee2024}; and (3) convergent evolution.

This paper adopts a third path, avoiding the anthropomorphic trap of inferring \textit{phenomenology} (what it ``feels like'') \citep{lee2024} and instead inferring \textit{functional-computational processes}. We cannot know \textit{what} a bat ``dreams'', but we can observe that it (a) has REM sleep and (b) its brain \textit{replays} neural ``flight trajectories'' \citep{omer2025batreplay}. The strongest evidence for this paper's thesis is the octopus. The fact that a mollusc, separated by over 550 million years of evolution, \textit{independently} evolved a two-stage ``quiet/active'' sleep architecture \citep{medeiros2023octopus} provides profound support for the Overfitted Brain Hypothesis. It strongly implies that a two-stage, NREM/REM-like learning algorithm (Consolidate/Generalize) is not a mammalian quirk but a \textit{fundamental, universal, and convergent solution} to the problem of learning in any complex, plastic nervous system.
