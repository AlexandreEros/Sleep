\section{Stage-Selective Deficits}\label{sec:stage_deficits}

The catastrophic failure from sleep deprivation (TSD) underscores sleep's non-negotiable role. TSD creates a cascade of cognitive and affective deficits:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive Deficits:} TSD selectively impairs ``executive function'' \- the high-level prefrontal tasks \- as well as attention networks \citep{ren2025, lim2024}. This leads to severe impairments in decision-making, which often becomes more impulsive and risk-prone \citep{lim2024}. Even chronic, partial sleep restriction results in cumulative cognitive impairment, suggesting the brain does not ``adapt'' to sleep loss \citep{ren2025}.
    \item \textbf{Affective Deficits:} The descent into psychosis (Section~\ref{sec:psychosis}) is a symptom of a profound failure of emotional regulation. TSD leads to ``elevated anxiety levels, impaired emotional regulation'', and an ``increased susceptibility to stress and depressive symptoms'' \citep{shah2025, oleary2017}. The brain's limbic system becomes disinhibited, leading to the emotional instability and paranoia seen in acute psychosis \citep{shah2025}.
\end{itemize}

More illuminating than TSD, however, are studies on \textit{stage-selective deprivation}, which use targeted awakenings to ``lesion'' a specific sleep stage. This evidence provides a ``smoking gun'' for the functional duality of NREM and REM sleep.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage-Specific Deficits:} Experimental results show a clear division of labor. Selectively suppressing SWS (N3) primarily impairs the consolidation of \textit{declarative memory} (e.g., word-pair lists). In contrast, selectively suppressing REM sleep primarily impairs the consolidation of \textit{procedural, motor, and emotional memory} \citep{genzel2014}.
    \item \textbf{Stage-Specific Rebound:} The most compelling evidence comes from recovery nights. The brain does not simply ``get more sleep''; it preferentially and aggressively seeks to recover the \textit{specific stage it lost}. After TSD, the brain prioritizes \textbf{SWS rebound} first, reflecting the ``foundational'' need for the maintenance described in Section~\ref{sec:maintenance_trio} \citep{benington1999}. Following \textit{selective} REM deprivation, the brain exhibits a ``rising REM sleep propensity'', meaning it takes more and more interventions to prevent REM sleep onset. On the subsequent recovery night, the brain enters a massive ``REM rebound'', increasing REM sleep time to as much as 140\% of its normal baseline \citep{endo1998}.
\end{itemize}

This non-negotiable biological mandate \- the need for \textit{both} the NREM consolidation phase and the REM generalization phase \- provides the crucial scientific foundation for Part III, which will explore the computational \textit{reason} for this two-part algorithm: to solve the fundamental problem of overfitting.
